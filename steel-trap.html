BUILDING BLOCKS: MYELIN, AXONS & WHAT THEY'RE MADE OF

Myelin is the insulating sheath that wraps axons and lets signals travel fast and cleanly. It is mostly lipid (roughly 70–80% by dry weight): cholesterol, phospholipids, and galactocerebrosides, with the rest protein — mainly myelin basic protein (MBP) and proteolipid protein (PLP). In the brain it is made by oligodendrocytes, and it is remodeled throughout life — myelination is one of the slower, more structural forms of plasticity.

Axons and neurons are built from the same raw materials as other cells, specialized: a phospholipid bilayer membrane studded with ion channels and receptors (cholesterol-rich, with DHA-heavy phospholipids in signaling membranes), a cytoskeleton of microtubules and neurofilaments that also serves as the transport rail for materials, and dense mitochondria — signaling is energetically expensive.

What the body needs to build and maintain them: dietary cholesterol and fats (the brain makes much of its own cholesterol, but the lipid supply chain matters), vitamin B12 and folate (B12 deficiency directly damages myelin — a classic, reversible-if-caught cause of neurological symptoms), iron (oligodendrocytes are iron-hungry), iodine and thyroid hormone (required for myelination, especially in development), omega-3 DHA (a major structural fatty acid of neuronal membranes), choline (for phosphatidylcholine membranes), adequate protein and energy, and sleep, during which maintenance and metabolite clearance run.

Synapses and neurotransmitters, briefly: synaptic vesicles are phospholipid spheres; release machinery is protein. The transmitters themselves are built from dietary precursors — acetylcholine from choline, dopamine/norepinephrine from tyrosine (iron and B6 as cofactors), serotonin from tryptophan, and GABA/glutamate cycling through glucose metabolism. Adequacy of the basics — protein, choline, B-vitamins, iron — is what the evidence supports; no food has been shown to selectively supercharge a single transmitter in a healthy person.

General education, honestly framed: these are established composition facts and deficiency consequences. They do not mean eating more of any single nutrient measurably grows myelin in a healthy adult — deficiency avoidance is the proven part.
